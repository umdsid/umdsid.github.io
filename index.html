I am a mathematical biologist and computational scientist interested in how living systems organize, transmit, and control information. My research moves between mathematical theory and biological measurement, from spatial transcriptomics and three-dimensional tissue mapping to stochastic networks and the geometry of neural population dynamics. I am particularly interested in how molecular state, spatial organization, and network architecture combine to produce larger-scale biological dynamics, communication, and control.

My work draws on spatial omics, computational neuroscience, network science, stochastic processes, optimal transport, information geometry, and geometric measure theory. Across these areas, I develop computational methods that connect high-dimensional biological measurements to interpretable mathematical structure. A recurring question is whether biological efficiency lies not only in the properties of individual components, but in the architecture of their interactions. Ultimately, I aim to identify principles of biological organization that can inform both mechanistic biology and new learning systems.

Research

Mathematical organization in living systems

I study how biological systems build large-scale organization from local interactions: how information propagates, how sparse networks coordinate physiology, and how tissue relationships change with observational scale.

01

Geometry and stochastic dynamics of biological information

How should information propagation in a living network be represented mathematically?

I develop stochastic and geometric descriptions of collective biological dynamics, particularly neural population activity. Current work constructs causal avalanches from networks of càdlàg processes and studies their realizations in physical spacetime and information space. The program connects causal event graphs and random measures with information geometry, affine line families, multiscale incidence geometry, and Kakeya-type questions.

Stochastic processes · random measures · information geometry · geometric measure theory

02

Network architecture, physiology and control

How can sparse biological networks coordinate physiology across large spatial scales?

I study neural and neurovascular networks as architectures for distributed sensing, communication, and control. Using neural population recordings, three-dimensional human kidney imaging, network inference, and physiological modeling, I investigate how heterogeneous connectivity allows local units to influence larger-scale state. Current work asks whether heavy-tailed innervation provides a scalable access architecture shared across brain and kidney without treating heavy-tailed structure as automatic evidence of equilibrium criticality.

Network science · computational neuroscience · connectomics · nonequilibrium control

03

Multiscale organization of spatial tissue

What persists, and what reorganizes, when biological tissue is represented across scale?

I develop computational frameworks for spatial transcriptomics that preserve relationships among molecular state, tissue geometry, topology, mechanics, and directional intercellular communication as cellular organization is represented across scale. SUTRA—Spatial Unified Transcriptomic Reconstruction and Analysis (सूत्र)—constructs an auditable multiscale hierarchy while retaining cellular ancestry and biological provenance. The framework uses pooled contextual scales together with specimen-local decisions, allowing shared structure to be compared across tissues without erasing specimen-specific biological organization.

Spatial transcriptomics · tissue mechanics · directed communication · multiscale statistics

Longer-term direction

These programs converge on a broader question: whether mathematical principles found in living systems—sparse heterogeneous access, causal propagation, multiscale representation, and distributed control—can provide foundations for learning architectures that operate under stringent physical and energetic constraints.
